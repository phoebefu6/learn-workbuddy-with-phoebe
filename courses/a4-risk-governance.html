Risk and governance

a3 gave you a pilot shortlist. Before anyone runs it, tonight answers the two questions your CISO, your legal counsel and your own gut will ask: what can this agent touch, and where does our data go? The good news is that WorkBuddy ships with a governance surface built in - three permission layers, a local-first data model, a Data Management page, an editable private Memory. The leader's job is not to build controls from scratch; it is to know what to ask at each layer, set one non-negotiable shipping rule, and write it all down as an eight-question policy your team can actually follow. Still no tool opened - this is policy work.

Part 0

Governance is questions, not paranoia

Agent risk conversations usually fail in one of two directions: blanket bans that push usage underground, or blanket enthusiasm that discovers the risk surface in a postmortem. The middle path is boring and it works: know the controls the product already has, decide who holds each one, and make the rules explicit before the pilot starts. Part 1 · covers folder authorization, plan confirmation, sandboxed execution + audit trail

Three layers between an instruction and your files 9 min live

Operators learn these layers as setup steps; leaders should read them as a governance surface. Each layer is a control the product provides and a discipline your team can keep or erode. The product default is safe; the risk is human - broad grants to escape prompts, rubber-stamped plans, audit trails nobody opens. So attach one question to each layer and ask it in every pilot review.

LiveThe one non-negotiable: verify before external use6 min▶

Every source, official and independent, converges on one sentence: agent output needs verification before it leaves the building. Here is the story that turns that sentence into policy - it comes from the operator track's simulator (session b2), built on the course's running project:

Meridian, a Singapore tea brand, assembles a market-entry pack. The Malaysia pilot file is priced in MYR; the Singapore books are in SGD. The instruction was good - clear goal, right files, right format, right Expert. One clause was missing: a verify ask. The agent summed ringgit straight into Singapore dollars and delivered a pack totalling S$102,947.00 - clean, formatted, confident, and wrong by 3.3x on the Malaysia line. With the verify clause on, the cross-foot check catches it: RM 18,985.00 converts to S$5,753.03, corrected total S$89,715.03, audit trail attached.

Read what that story is really saying to a leader:

  • The dangerous failure is silent. Wrong topic, missing data and bad formatting all announce themselves. An unverified number looks exactly like a verified one - until an investor checks it.
  • The trap was in the data, not the tool. No vendor fix eliminates this class of error; a verification step surfaces it. That is why this is policy, not a product setting.
  • The cost of the control is one sentence. "Cross-check every figure against the source and attach the audit trail" - one clause per instruction, plus a named human who owns the check for anything external.

The policy line for your doc: no agent-produced figure or document goes to a client, regulator, investor or the public without a named person verifying it against the audit trail. Internal drafts move fast; external artifacts cross a gate. One rule, two speeds.

Part 2 · covers the local-first desktop model, Data Management, Memory, connectors

Where does our data actually go? 8 min live

The question every security review opens with - and WorkBuddy's answer is unusually clean at the start: it is a desktop app that works on local files, with no mandatory cloud upload - one of its genuine differentiators. The picture changes the moment connectors enter: each connector (Google Drive, Gmail, Notion, Slack, GitHub, Jira) is its own data decision with its own flow. Govern the two paths separately and the conversation with your CISO gets much shorter.

Real world

The CISO conversation, compressed. "Where does the data go?" has a two-sentence answer once the paths are separated: on the local path, files stay on the laptop - no mandatory cloud upload. Connectors change that, so each one arrives at the review as its own line item with its own data flow. Reviews that start this way approve pilots; reviews that start with "it's an AI agent" stall.

Self-studyMarketplace vetting + permission modes: the org dials4 min read▶

Two more governance dials worth setting deliberately before a5's rollout:

  • Skills Marketplace vetting. The marketplace ships skill packages your team can install, and it runs automatic security scanning on them - a real control, and not a sufficient one. Treat marketplace skills exactly like your browser-extension policy: scanning catches the known-bad, a human still asks "who published this, what does it access, do we need it?" A simple rule that works: anyone may propose a skill, one named person approves installs for the team, and the approved list lives in the policy doc.
  • Permission modes as the org dial. The product exposes permission modes - how much the agent may do between confirmations. This is your rollout throttle: start every new user conservative (confirm plans, confirm outward actions), and loosen per task type only after a pattern has earned trust in pilot reviews. The dial should move on evidence, not on enthusiasm - and moving it should be a logged decision, not a personal preference. Scheduled automation (operator session b8) is where a loosened dial matters most, so decide the rule before anyone schedules anything.

UI names for both surfaces can drift between releases - re-verify at workbuddy.ai before the policy doc goes out.

Exercise

Draft the eight-question governance checklist ◐ 18 min in session + this week

Answer these eight questions in writing and you have the WorkBuddy section of your team policy doc. Each maps to a control you met tonight; none needs more than three sentences.

  • 1 · Folders. Which folders may the agent touch, per role - and who approves widening access beyond them?
  • 2 · Plans. Who confirms plans before execution, and is rubber-stamping named in the doc as a violation, not a shortcut?
  • 3 · Modes. What is the default permission mode for new users, and what evidence justifies loosening it for a task type?
  • 4 · Verification. What counts as "external use" for us, and who is the named verifier for each artifact class that crosses that line?
  • 5 · Connectors. Which connectors are approved, what data does each one move, and who can revoke one?
  • 6 · Audit trails. Who reviews audit trails, on what cadence, and where do they note what they found?
  • 7 · Marketplace. Who approves skill installs, and where does the approved-skill list live?
  • 8 · Data hygiene. When is the Data Management page reviewed, what is our retention stance, and what must never go into Memory?
Keep it to one page A governance doc nobody reads governs nothing. Eight answers, one page, reviewed quarterly - and revisit answers 3, 5 and 7 after a5, when the Team plan's Admin Console gives you central enforcement instead of per-seat trust.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why did the S$102,947.00 pack nearly ship, when the instruction was otherwise excellent?

The trap sat in the data (MYR summed as SGD), and nothing failed loudly. One verify clause surfaced it: RM 18,985.00 → S$5,753.03, corrected total S$89,715.03. That is why verification is policy, not preference.

2 · A team member wants to connect Gmail "since we already approved Google Drive". The right governance response is:

The local-first path and each connector get different treatments. Drive approval says nothing about what Gmail access moves across the boundary. Per-connector, named, revocable approvals - question 5 on your checklist.

3 · The Skills Marketplace runs automatic security scanning. What does that change about your vetting policy?

Scanning is a real control and an insufficient one - the same posture you already hold on browser-extension stores. Keep the human approval step: who published it, what does it access, do we need it?
