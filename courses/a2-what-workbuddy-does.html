Part 1 · covers Tencent Cloud "What WorkBuddy Can Do", Install & Use guide

The four-stage loop, in leader terms 9 min live

Every WorkBuddy task runs the same loop: Understand the plain-language ask, Plan the steps - and pause for human confirmation, Act inside a sandbox limited to authorized folders, Deliver a file plus an audit trail of every change. For a leader the loop matters less as engineering and more as a control diagram: it has exactly two built-in points where your people exercise judgment, and neither is optional. That symmetry - approve the plan going in, verify the deliverable coming out - is the entire governance story in miniature, and a4 builds on it directly.

LiveBehind the menu - what your team actually gets5 min▶

The delegation menu in Part 2 is the surface. Behind it, three layers do the lifting - worth knowing at name level so vendor claims and team requests both make sense:

  • 100+ built-in Experts - specialist agents for market research, financial analysis, slide design, legal drafting and more. Naming the right Expert in an instruction noticeably changes output quality; operators learn this in b5.
  • 20+ skill packages - reusable skill sets your team can compose into repeatable workflows, plus a Skills Marketplace with automatic security scanning (the vetting story matters for a5's rollout).
  • Connectors - GitHub, Jira, Confluence, Google Drive, Gmail, Notion, Slack. Each one widens what the agent can reach, which is a capability AND a data decision; a4 covers the governance side, b7 the hands-on side.
  • Chat-platform delivery - scheduled results push to Slack, Telegram, Discord, WeChat Work, Feishu, DingTalk or QQ, so deliverables land where your team already lives.

Feature inventories drift on a product this young - re-verify counts and connector lists at workbuddy.ai before quoting them in a deck.

Self-studyThe demo story to retell - Meridian's one-pager4 min read▶

You need one concrete story for meetings. Borrow this one - it comes from the operator track's b2 session, where it runs live in a simulator over the course's teaching dataset. Meridian is the course's running case: a Singapore specialty-tea brand entering Malaysia.

  • Same task, twice. An analyst needs a leadership one-pager on H1 sales. First attempt, the vague ask: "do something with the sales data." Back comes a polished six-page trend report - about the wrong question, with no headline number anyone asked for. Straight to the bin.
  • Then the sharp ask: analyze H1 sales for Monday's leadership sync, from the named spreadsheet, as a one-pager - headline number, top product, three bullets, one risk - and cross-check every figure against the source before delivering.
  • What lands: one page, on time, with the Q2 revenue figure - S$46,332.00 - verified against the source sheet, top product named, risks flagged. Same agent, same data, opposite outcomes.

The moral you say out loud: the tool did not get smarter between attempt one and attempt two - the instruction did. That is why instruction craft is a team skill worth training, not a nice-to-have. (Honesty note when retelling: the numbers come from the course's teaching simulation over its own dataset, not from a WorkBuddy benchmark.)

Exercise · pen and paper

Write one instruction for your team's most common chore ◐ 15 min · no tool

Leaders who have written one good instruction themselves make better delegation decisions than leaders who have only seen demos. Pick the most common chore from your a1 audit sheet and draft the ask - on paper, never in the tool.

Pick the chore. The most frequent file-bound task from your audit - the weekly report, the folder cleanup, the recurring comparison. Frequency beats glamour: a chore done 50 times a year is worth 50 good instructions.

Draft it against the five-lever preview. The operator track's b2 teaches these in full; the leader version fits on a sticky note: clear goal (outcome + audience), context + files (which folder, which files), output format (type, length, structure), right Expert (name the specialty if obvious), verify ask (cross-check figures, attach the trail).

Read it back as the agent would. Every unstated detail is a guess you just delegated. If a smart temp on day one could misread your draft, so can the agent - tighten until they could not.

Keep the draft. It becomes a pilot candidate in a5, and if you ever walk the operator track, it is your b2 test case. For the full five-lever treatment with the live simulator, point your team at b2.

Why leaders write one instruction Not to become operators - to calibrate. After writing one real instruction you can tell in seconds whether a team member's "the agent got it wrong" is a tool problem or an instruction problem. That judgment is the cheapest management upgrade this course offers.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Where do the two human checkpoints sit in the four-stage loop?

Automation between two human gates: your person approves the steps going in and verifies the file coming out. Not zero supervision, not constant babysitting - two well-placed checkpoints, which a4 turns into governance.

2 · A team member asks what WorkBuddy could take off their plate. Which list is the delegation menu?

Five documented families, straight from Tencent Cloud's capability guide. B is a different product category entirely (this course is office work, not coding), and C is how pilots die - tasks outside the families are your "not agent work yet" answer.

3 · In the Meridian story, the same task produced a binned six-pager and then a usable one-pager. What changed?

Same agent, same data, opposite outcomes - only the instruction changed. That is the retellable moral, and why instruction craft (b2 for your team, the sticky-note version for you) is a trainable skill, not luck.
