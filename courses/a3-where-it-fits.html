Part 0

The question tonight actually answers

"Should we use an agent?" is the wrong question - too big to answer and too vague to fail. The right question is per-task: for this specific recurring chore, is delegation to an agent cheaper, safer and better than doing it by hand? Tonight gives you two instruments for answering it at scale: a triage 2x2 you can apply to any task in thirty seconds, and a week-mapping exercise that finds the agent-shaped hours hiding in your team's calendar. WorkBuddy's own capability guide gives us the delegation menu; your team's real week gives us the demand side.

Part 1 · built on Tencent Cloud's documented task families + independent 2026 reviews

Delegate or DIY: the two axes that decide 10 min live

Two properties of a task predict almost everything about how delegation goes. Ambiguity: how many reasonable interpretations does the instruction leave open? Stakes: what does a wrong or mediocre result cost before someone catches it? Cross them and you get four postures - not "yes or no to AI", but four different ways of working with an agent, each with its own supervision price.

LiveWhat agents are genuinely bad at - the four keeps6 min▶

The honest edges, from Tencent's own docs and the independent reviews. These four never move out of the top-right quadrant, and a leader's job is to say so out loud before anyone over-delegates:

  • Goals. An agent executes the goal it is given; it cannot tell you whether that goal is worth pursuing, for whom, or at what standard. Every misfire the operator track studies traces back to a goal the human never made explicit.
  • Taste. Output can satisfy every stated constraint and still read as generic. The craft nobody wrote down - what your clients consider "us" - stays a human contribution, even when an Expert narrows the gap.
  • Accountability. A deliverable can be generated; responsibility cannot. When a figure ships to a client, a named person on your team vouched for it. "The agent did it" is not an answer you ever want to hear in a postmortem, so design the workflow so it can never be the answer.
  • Novel judgment. Agents excel where patterns exist. The first time a situation occurs - a new regulation, a surprise competitor move, an unusual client request - there is no pattern to execute, and pattern-shaped output about a novel situation is quietly dangerous.

Notice what this list is NOT: it is not "agents are bad at spreadsheets" or "agents make things up so avoid them". The documented task families - documents, data analysis, research, file operations, scheduled automation - are genuinely strong. The line runs between execution and judgment, not between task types.

Part 2 · the demand side: your team's calendar

Mapping a real week onto the task families 8 min live

The capability guide names five task families: documents and reports, data analysis, research, file operations, scheduled automation. The useful move is to hold that menu against a real week. Below are three illustrative 40-hour weeks - an analyst, a marketer, an ops coordinator. The pattern repeats across roles: a quarter to a third of the week is agent-shaped - recurring, low-ambiguity, checkable - and it is rarely the hours people think of first. The judgment hours stay untouched, and that is the point: delegation buys those hours back.

Real world

The hours people name first are the wrong ones. Ask a team "what should the agent do?" and they nominate their hardest work - the strategy doc, the tricky analysis - because that is where the pain is. The 2x2 says the opposite: the win is in the boring bottom-left hours nobody defends. Delegate the merge, keep the meaning.

Self-studyThe pilot shortlist method - three tasks, three filters4 min read▶

When you move from mapping to piloting (a5 builds the full rollout), resist the temptation to pilot the most impressive task. Pilot the most provable one. Pick exactly three recurring tasks that pass all three filters:

  • High frequency. Weekly or better. A monthly task gives you one data point per month; a pilot needs repetitions to show a trend and to let instructions improve between runs.
  • Clear success criteria. You can say, before the run, what a correct result looks like - "all Q1 files merged, totals match the source sheets". If success is a matter of opinion, the pilot will end in a matter of opinion.
  • Low blast radius. A wrong result costs an internal redo, not a client apology. Bottom-left of the 2x2 only. The stakes ladder is for later, after the verify habit exists (a4 makes it policy).

Three is deliberate: one task can be a fluke, five dilutes attention. Three tasks, run weekly for a month, each with a named operator and a written success criterion, produce the only slide a6 needs: hours saved, error rate, and whether the team asked to keep it.

One honesty note: task-family names and capabilities drift as WorkBuddy ships updates (global launch was May 2026) - re-verify the current menu at workbuddy.ai before you commit the shortlist.

Exercise

Build your team's pilot shortlist ◐ 18 min in session + this week

  • List ten. From real calendars, inboxes and standing meetings, list ten recurring tasks your team did last week. Chores count double - the boring ones are usually the delegable ones.
  • Plot them. Place each on the ambiguity-x-stakes 2x2 from Part 1. Argue about placements - the argument IS the value; it surfaces what your team believes about stakes.
  • Filter to three. From the bottom-left quadrant, keep the three that pass all three shortlist filters: weekly-or-better frequency, a success criterion you can write in one sentence, internal-only blast radius.
  • Write the criteria. One sentence per task: what does a correct result look like, and who checks it? If you cannot write the sentence, the task goes back in the pool.
  • Name the operators. One person per task who will actually run it - and send them to operator sessions b1 and b2 before the pilot starts. Bring the shortlist to a4: next session wraps it in the governance it needs.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A monthly board pack with figures that go to investors sits where on the triage?

The task is low-ambiguity (the format and sources repeat monthly) but high-stakes (errors escape to investors). That quadrant's posture is delegation WITH a mandatory named verifier - not avoidance, and not blind trust.

2 · Why does the pilot shortlist demand weekly-or-better frequency?

A pilot is an experiment, and experiments need sample size. Four to five runs in a month show whether results improve as the operator's instructions sharpen - a monthly task would take half a year to show the same curve.

3 · Which of these belongs on the four-keeps list - things the leader never delegates?

Goals, taste, accountability, novel judgment - the four keeps. Merging files is classic delegate-now; a draft scan is delegate-to-draft. The line runs between execution and judgment, not between task types.
