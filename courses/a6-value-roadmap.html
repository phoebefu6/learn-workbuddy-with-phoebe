Part 0

The session where numbers meet judgment

Every failed AI rollout has a dashboard, and the dashboard is green. That is because most rollouts measure what the tool makes easy to count - logins, tasks run, messages sent - instead of what the business actually wanted: work that ships and hours that come back. Tonight installs the discipline once: a short list of metrics that cannot be gamed by enthusiasm, one piece of cost arithmetic that survives a CFO, and a decision rule you commit to before the pilot report lands on your desk.

Part 1 · the measurement half of a5's pilot

Metrics that are not vanity 10 min live

The test is simple: a vanity metric can rise while zero value lands; a value metric cannot. Logins rise when people are curious. Tasks-run rises when people are playing. Neither tells you whether Monday's briefing was actually read or the analysis actually reached the client. Your pilot tracks three value metrics and nothing else: deliverables used downstream, hours returned on the three pilot tasks, and the rework rate on agent output.

LiveWhen to scale, when to stop6 min▶

Write the rule before the report exists, so the decision is a reading, not a negotiation:

  • Scale when both hold: pilot deliverables are being used weekly by their downstream consumers, and the verify discipline is holding - audit trails read, figures cross-checked before anything leaves the building (a4's non-negotiable, b10 on the operator side).
  • Stop (or redesign) when outputs are re-done by hand more than 30% of the time. At that rework rate you are paying twice for the same work - once in credits, once in human hours - and the honest fixes are better instructions or a different task, not more seats.

Two traps between those lines. Scaling on enthusiasm: the demo wowed, the champions are happy, the left column is green - but nothing is being used weekly yet; that is "extend the pilot", not "scale". Stopping on one bad week: a single rework spike usually traces to one bad instruction template - diagnose with b2's five levers before you kill a task. And scaling itself stays boring: add the next three tasks and next three people, re-run the same 3 x 3 x 3 with the same bar. The playbook does not change shape because it worked.

Part 2 · the arithmetic your CFO will actually ask for

Credit economics per deliverable 8 min live

One number decides the budget conversation: what does a used deliverable cost, and what did the hour-cost it replaced look like? The Team plan gives you clean inputs - ~$40 per seat per month and a 1,000-Credit shared pool metered in the Admin Console (a5). Divide the month's seat cost by the month's used deliverables. Not produced - used. Vanity accounting sneaks back in through the denominator.

LiveKeeping the arithmetic honest4 min▶

Three adjustments separate a credible number from a slide-ware number:

  • Count verification time as cost. The verify discipline (a4) is human minutes per deliverable. Add it to the agent side of the ledger before comparing - the comparison usually still wins, and now it survives scrutiny.
  • Charge rework to the agent. A deliverable redone by hand counts its full human hours on the agent side. This is what connects the CFO math to the 30% stop rule - past that rate, cost per deliverable quietly exceeds the hour-cost it was meant to replace.
  • Watch credits per task family in the console. Credit burn differs by task type, and heavy research or deck tasks eat disproportionately. If the pool empties before month-end, your true cost includes the top-up - price it in rather than discovering it in Q3.
Self-studyThe roadmap: what changes next in agent land4 min read▶

What you decided on tonight is a moving target, and the honest read of the direction of travel:

  • Deeper connectors. The Drive / Gmail / Notion / Slack / GitHub / Jira set follows the pluggable MCP pattern, so expect the reachable-context list to keep growing - and with it, the data-governance surface you scoped in a4. Each new connector is a governance decision, not a free upgrade.
  • More Experts and skills. The 100+ Expert catalog and 20+ skill packages are the fastest-drifting inventory in the product. Your a5 vetting workflow is built for exactly this - reuse it as the catalog grows.
  • Scheduled and remote patterns maturing. Scheduled runs pushing to Slack, Telegram, Discord and the other platforms, plus phone-triggered tasks, are where agents shift from tool to infrastructure. That is also where permission modes deserve a fresh look, because unattended runs raise the stakes on a4's boundary.
  • The honest note: the global edition is young - worldwide launch was May 2026 - and it is moving fast. International support still lags the China edition, UI names drift between updates, and plans change. Put a quarterly re-verify in the calendar: pricing, connector list, permission surface, marketplace policy. Thirty minutes a quarter keeps every number in your decision memo true.

Exercise · 25-42 in session

Write your scale/stop decision memo template ◐ 17 min

The memo you will fill in at week 3 of the pilot - written now, while no result can bias it. Five blocks, one page.

The three value metrics, with blanks. Deliverables used downstream (per task, per week): ___. Hours returned on the three pilot tasks: ___. Rework rate: ___%. No other numbers admitted.

The cost line. Seat spend / used deliverables = $___ each, verification time added, rework hours charged to the agent side, versus the hour-cost replaced: $___.

The pre-committed rule, verbatim. "Scale if deliverables are used weekly AND verify discipline is holding. Stop or redesign if rework exceeds 30%." Sign it today's date - that signature is the whole point.

The scale shape. If scale: the next 3 tasks, next 3 people, same 3 weeks, same bar. If stop: which lever failed (instruction, task choice, or tool) and what would have to change to retry.

The re-verify footnote. "Pricing, connectors and permission surface re-verified at workbuddy.ai on [date]; next quarterly check due [date]." Then file the template next to a5's one-pager - they travel together.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Which of these belongs in the decision memo?

Seats and tasks-run are activity: they can climb while zero value lands. Rework rate is an outcome metric that can move against you - which is exactly what makes it trustworthy, and what wires the memo to the 30% stop rule.

2 · In the cost-per-deliverable arithmetic, the denominator is:

$120 of seats over 20 USED deliverables is $6 each - a number that survives a CFO. Divide by everything produced and vanity accounting is back through the denominator. And keep it honest: verification time and rework hours go on the agent's side of the ledger.

3 · The pilot deliverables are used weekly and verify discipline is holding, but rework sits at 35%. The memo says:

The 30% line was pre-committed precisely for this moment: at 35% rework, cost per deliverable quietly exceeds the hour-cost it replaced. Diagnose with the five levers (usually an instruction fix) and retry - but the rule reads, it does not negotiate.
