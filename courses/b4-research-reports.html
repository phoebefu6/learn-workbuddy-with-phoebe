Multi-source research - the goal lever's home ground 7 min live

The capability guide's promise: WorkBuddy compiles multi-source research into organized reports and competitive matrices. Sources means the web plus every file you have authorized - and later, after b7, your Drive and Notion too. But compilation has no opinion about relevance. Point it at "the Malaysia tea market" and you get everything: history, festivals, plantation tourism. Point it at a decision - retail-first or online-first - and every section it writes has a job. The decision question is the filter that turns sources into a report.

LiveTrivia vs decision support - same sources, different goal clause4 min▶

Two instructions over identical sources:

  • "Research the Malaysia tea market." Returns a tidy encyclopedia entry: consumption history, brand lists, festival calendars. Nothing wrong with any sentence; nothing you can act on either. The goal-off signature from b2, wearing a research costume.
  • "Should Meridian enter Malaysia via retail or online first? Research the market to answer that." Now channel economics matter, competitor channel choices matter, e-commerce penetration matters - and plantation tourism gets cut. The report argues toward a recommendation you can accept or reject.

The test before you press go: what will we do differently depending on what this comes back with? If you cannot answer, you are ordering trivia.

Self-studyWhat research can and cannot give you3 min read▶
  • It compiles; it does not warrant. Web sources vary in quality and age. That is why tonight's instructions demand footnoted sources - so YOU can judge what the report stands on.
  • Estimates are fine when labeled. Market-share numbers for private companies are almost always estimates. The sin is not estimating - it is presenting an estimate with the same face as a sourced fact.
  • Recency is a property, not a default. Ask for it: "prefer sources from the last 12 months, flag anything older." Research deliverables age fast - the callout after Build-along 2 makes this a rule.
  • The judgment stays yours. The report recommends; Meridian decides. Same boundary as b3's risk line, at bigger stakes.
Part 2 · the format lever's strictest test

The matrix discipline 6 min live

A competitive matrix is not four essays about four companies. It is a grid where every column asks the same question of every row - price band, channels, positioning, share estimate - so your eye can compare straight down. Ask for "a comparison of competitors" and you get prose blobs; the compilation was fine, the format clause was missing. Fix the columns in the instruction and the format lever does the rest.

LiveWhy columns beat prose3 min▶

Three things a real matrix does that essays cannot:

  • Exposes gaps. An empty cell is visible; a missing fact inside a paragraph is invisible. If nobody can find Cameron & Co.'s channel mix, the matrix says so - honestly, with a blank or an "estimate".
  • Forces the same question everywhere. Essays drift: one competitor gets a history lesson, another gets pricing detail. Columns do not drift.
  • Feeds the next artifact. b5's launch deck will lift the positioning column straight into a slide. Prose blobs feed nothing.

The instruction move: never ask for "a comparison". Name the rows AND the columns, and say what an unfillable cell should contain.

Self-studyChoosing columns that earn their place2 min read▶

Columns come from the decision question, not from habit. Tonight's four, and why each serves "retail or online first":

  • Price band - where Meridian's S$16-S$32 range lands against locals tells you which shelf it fights for.
  • Channels - the decision column: who already wins in retail, who owns online, where the gap is.
  • Positioning - heritage, value, gifting: what story is already taken.
  • Share estimate - rough sizing of who you would displace. Almost always "estimate" - labeled as such.

A fifth column is fine if the question demands it. A seventh usually means the question is fuzzy - go back to the goal clause.

Build-along 1 of 2

The Malaysia market report - pack artifact 2 ★ 12 min · everyone does it

One decision question, a named research scope, a pinned report shape. This is the five-lever pattern from b2 and b3 with the context lever now reaching the open web.

Instruct. "Research whether Meridian should enter Malaysia via retail or online first. Scope: Malaysia specialty-tea market - size, channel split, e-commerce penetration, competitor channel choices - using web sources plus ~/Documents/Meridian/meridian-pack/01-sg-sales-analysis as our baseline. Format: exec summary stating the recommendation, then 3 sections (market + channels, competitor landscape, entry options + risks), every claim footnoted to its source with a date, estimates labeled as estimates. Prefer sources from the last 12 months."

Read the plan. It should list web research steps AND your b3 artifact. If the plan skips your baseline file, the report will float free of Meridian's actual numbers - tighten and re-confirm.

Confirm and let it compile. Multi-source research takes longer than b3's merge - this is the session's coffee moment, not a hang.

Verify the skeleton first. Exec summary answers the question in its first line? Three sections, each pulling toward the recommendation? Sources footnoted with dates? Shape first, then content.

Then verify the load-bearing claims. Pick the three claims the recommendation leans on hardest and open their footnoted sources yourself. If a source does not say what the report says it says, you have found tonight's most valuable lesson.

File it as meridian-pack/02-my-market-report, with today's date in the document header.

The recommendation is an input, not a verdict The report will land on retail-first or online-first with reasons. Read it as a well-argued brief from a fast analyst - your job is to interrogate it, not to obey it. The leader track spends a whole session (a3) on this boundary; operators live it one artifact at a time.
Build-along 2 of 2

The competitor matrix - pack artifact 3 ★ 12 min · everyone does it

Rows and columns fixed in the instruction, one row of your own choosing, and a verify ask that turns sourcing into a per-cell rule.

Pick your fourth competitor. Sixty seconds of web search: find one more Malaysia tea player beyond the course's three. Real research means your matrix will not match your neighbor's - that is the point.

Instruct. "Build a competitor matrix for Meridian's Malaysia entry. Rows: Highland Leaf, TehMas, Cameron & Co., and [your pick]. Columns: price band, channels, positioning, share estimate - exactly these four. Every cell cites a source with a date or is marked 'estimate'. Cells that cannot be filled either way stay blank and are listed below the matrix as open questions."

Read the plan, confirm, and let it research - four companies, four questions each.

Verify cell by cell. Sixteen cells: each one has a source, says "estimate", or is blank-and-listed. Any confident-looking cell with none of the three is a silent wrong number in matrix clothing - send it back.

Read down the channels column and compare it against the report's recommendation. If the matrix and the report disagree about where the channel gap is, one of them is wrong - and you just caught it before b5 builds a deck on top.

File it as meridian-pack/03-competitor-matrix, dated. Two artifacts tonight; the pack is now three for three.

Real world

Research rots - date-stamp everything. A pricing page changes, a competitor pivots channels, a share estimate goes stale - and the report reads exactly as confident as the day it was written. Put the run date in every research deliverable's header, and when a month has passed, re-run the instruction rather than reuse the file. Re-running costs minutes; the instruction is saved and the sources refresh. Reusing stale research costs credibility - usually in the meeting where someone quotes the new price.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 25-35 min total

  • Run a research task on your own industry with a real decision question - a make-or-buy, a vendor shortlist, a market you are curious about professionally. All the levers: question, scope, report format, footnoted sources, dated.
  • Before running it, write down what you expect the recommendation to be. Compare after. Where the report surprised you, check the sources behind the surprise first.
  • Build one matrix from your world - competitors, vendors, even job options - with columns fixed in the instruction and the per-cell source-or-estimate rule.
  • Find one research document you or your team reused this year that was more than three months old. Check what changed. That is your date-stamp conversion story.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · "Research the Malaysia tea market" returns a tidy, accurate, useless report. Which lever failed?

Research without a decision question returns trivia - the goal-off signature at its most polished. The sources were reachable and the compilation was fine; nothing told the agent what to keep and what to cut.

2 · What makes a competitor matrix a matrix, rather than four essays?

Table formatting is cosmetic - you can pour four essays into four wide cells and compare nothing. Comparability comes from fixed columns named in the instruction: price band, channels, positioning, share estimate.

3 · A month from now, someone asks for tonight's market report. Best move?

Research rots and reads exactly as confident stale as fresh - that is why every deliverable carries its run date. The instruction is saved; re-running costs minutes. Hiding the date is manufacturing the silent wrong number on purpose.
